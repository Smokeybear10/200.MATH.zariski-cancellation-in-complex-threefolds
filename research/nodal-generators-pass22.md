# Five global generators of the fixed conductor surface

The fixed nodal surface from passes 20–21 has an explicit closed
embedding into \(\mathbb A^5\). Five polynomial functions generate
its actual coordinate ring. The proof combines interpolation,
finite-module algebra, three exact polynomial-gcd certificates, and a
local Nakayama argument. It does not prove that five is minimal, supply
an embedding into \(\mathbb A^3\), or realize an ambient quotient.

Two natural three-generator attempts are excluded below. They are
specific failed presentations, not an obstruction to every triple.
No mathematical priority or human expert review is claimed.

## 1. Normalize constants without changing the gluing

In the [fixed construction](nodal-conductor-pass20.md), let the
original variables be \(w,y,z_{\rm old}\), with \(a_0^4=3\).
Set
\[
u=w/a_0,\qquad z=a_0^2z_{\rm old}.
\]
Then its normalization is exactly
\[
B=\mathbb C[u,y,z]/(A(u)z-y(y-1)),\qquad
A(u)=(u-1)(u-i),\qquad d=u^4-1.
\tag{1}
\]
The old conductor polynomial is \(3d\). Multiplying the common
line parameter by \(a_0^3\) gives the normalized gluing
\[
t=\frac{z}{u_j(2\epsilon-1)}\text{ on a split line},
\qquad t=\frac{y}{u_jA(u_j)}\text{ on an unsplit line}.
\tag{2}
\]
This is one uniform rescaling on every line, not a new choice of
pairwise gluing. The pairs remain
\[
(L_{10},L_{30}),\quad(L_{11},L_4),\quad(L_2,L_{31}),
\]
over \(u_1=1,u_2=-1,u_3=i,u_4=-i\). Write \(R\subset B\)
for the *same pinched ring*, now in these normalized coordinates:
\[
R=B\times_{\mathbb C[t]^6}\mathbb C[t]^3,
\qquad I=dB=(d,du)R.
\tag{3}
\]
In particular, \(u\) belongs to the normalization \(B\), not
to \(R\). It is not an available algebra generator of \(R\).

## 2. The five explicit functions

Put \(m=(1+i)/2\), and define
\[
\begin{split}
a(u)&=1+i-\frac{1-i}{2}A(u),\\
b(u)&=-i(u-m)(u+1)(u+i),\\
p(u)&=-\frac{i}{8}uA(u).
\end{split}
\]
The trace and common-parameter lifts are
\[
c=a(u)+b(u)y,\qquad
\tau=\frac{i}{2}b(u)(2y-1)z+p(u)y.
\tag{4}
\]
For independent symbols \(C,T\), let
\[
\beta(C,T)=-\frac{iT}{2}
\left((1-i)C^2T-C^2-2CT+(1+i)C-2i\right).
\tag{5}
\]
Finally set
\[
\kappa=u^2-cu,\qquad
\zeta=z-\beta(c,\tau)u.
\tag{6}
\]

**Theorem.** The fixed pinched ring is
\[
\boxed{R=\mathbb C[c,\tau,\zeta,\kappa,d].}
\tag{7}
\]
These are five algebra generators, not five independent coordinates.

First check that they belong to \(R\). Their restrictions on the
three pairs, in the shared parameter (2), are:

| Pair | \(c\) | \(\tau\) | \(\kappa\) | \(\zeta\) | \(d\) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | \(1+i\) | \(t\) | \(-i\) | \(0\) | \(0\) |
| 2 | \(1-i\) | \(t\) | \(i\) | \((1+i)t-2it^2\) | \(0\) |
| 3 | \(-1+i\) | \(t\) | \(i\) | \((1+i)t+2it^2\) | \(0\) |

The two members of each pair give the same row. The polynomial
\(\beta\) interpolates their normalized \(z\)-slopes: at the
three displayed \(c\)-values it is respectively \(-t\),
\(i(2t^2-t)\), and \(-2t^2+it\).

## 3. Finiteness is global and explicit

Write \(S=\mathbb C[c,\tau,\zeta,\kappa,d]\subseteq R\).
The polynomial Bézout identity
\[
U(u)b(u)+V(u)p(u)=1
\tag{8}
\]
holds for
\[
\begin{split}
U(u)&=(1+i)u^2-\tfrac32iu-1+i,\\
V(u)&=-8(1+i)u^2-12iu+6-6i.
\end{split}
\]
Indeed (4) gives
\[
by=c-a,\qquad
py=\tau-iz(c-a)+\frac i2bz.
\]
Thus
\[
y=U(c-a)+V\left(\tau-iz(c-a)+\tfrac i2bz\right),
\tag{9}
\]
with all coefficient polynomials evaluated at \(u\). Since
\(z=\zeta+\beta(c,\tau)u\), this proves
\[
B=S[u].
\tag{10}
\]
Also \(u^2-cu-\kappa=0\), so \(B\) is generated by \(1,u\)
as an \(S\)-module. No module-freeness is asserted. The intermediate
ring \(R\) is a submodule of this finite module over the Noetherian
ring \(S\), so \(R\) is finite over \(S\).

It remains to prove that \(\operatorname{Spec}R\to\operatorname{Spec}S\)
is bijective on closed points and surjective on local cotangent
spaces. Both requirements matter; finiteness alone is insufficient.

## 4. Exact separation of global points

Suppose two normalization points have the same five values in (7).
If their \(u\)-values agree, (9)–(10) recover their \(y,z\), so
they are the same point. Otherwise equality of \(\kappa,d\) forces
\[
u'=\epsilon u,\qquad c=(1+\epsilon)u,\qquad
u\ne0,\qquad\epsilon\in\{-1,i,-i\}.
\tag{11}
\]
At \(d=0\), the restriction table gives exactly the three prescribed
pairs with the same common parameter \(\tau=t\). No other pair of
conductor lines has equal \(c\). These are precisely the existing
identifications defining \(R\).

Off \(d=0\), the only possible zero of \(b\) is \(u=m\).
There \(c=a(m)=3m/2\), contradicting (11), which would give
\(c=(1+\epsilon)m\). The case \(u'=m\) gives the same contradiction
with \(\epsilon^{-1}\). Hence both \(A\) and \(b\) are nonzero
at any remaining proposed collision.

Define rational functions in independent \(u,C\):
\[
Y(u,C)=\frac{C-a(u)}{b(u)},\qquad
Z(u,C)=\frac{Y(u,C)(Y(u,C)-1)}{A(u)},
\]
\[
T(u,C)=\frac i2b(u)(2Y(u,C)-1)Z(u,C)+p(u)Y(u,C).
\tag{12}
\]
For each \(\epsilon\in\{-1,i,-i\}\), substitute \(C=(1+\epsilon)u\)
and take the reduced numerators
\[
F_\epsilon(u)=\operatorname{num}
\bigl(T(u,C)-T(\epsilon u,C)\bigr),
\]
\[
G_\epsilon(u)=\operatorname{num}
\bigl(Z(u,C)-Z(\epsilon u,C)
 -(1-\epsilon)u\beta(C,T(u,C))\bigr).
\tag{13}
\]
The substitution for \(C\) occurs *after* replacing the first
argument by \(\epsilon u\); the two points have the same \(C\).
Over \(\mathbb Q(i)[u]\), the exact identities are
\[
\operatorname{monicgcd}(F_{-1},G_{-1})
=\operatorname{monicgcd}(F_i,G_i)
=\operatorname{monicgcd}(F_{-i},G_{-i})=u.
\tag{14}
\]
These are the finite computer-algebra certificates used in the proof.
The companion checker constructs (12)–(13) from their formulas and
verifies polynomial Bézout identities for all three gcds, not a
numerical root search. The original denominators are nonzero at
the points being considered. Equality of \(\tau,\zeta\) would
make both numerators vanish, so (14) would force \(u=0\), contrary
to (11).

Thus the finite map from \(\operatorname{Spec}R\) to
\(\operatorname{Spec}S\) is injective on closed points. It is
surjective because the extension is integral. All closed-point
residue fields are \(\mathbb C\).

## 5. Cotangent surjectivity, including every conductor point

Off the conductor the surface equals its smooth normalization.
If \(u\ne0\), then \(d_u=4u^3\ne0\). At fixed \(u\), either
\(c_y=b(u)\ne0\), or \(u=m\), where \(\tau_y=p(m)\ne0\).
Two listed functions therefore span the cotangent space there.

At \(u=0\), use \((u,c)\) as local coordinates since \(b(0)\ne0\).
If \(c\ne0\), then \(\partial\kappa/\partial u|_c=-c\ne0\).
If also \(c=0\), the point is \((u,y,z)=(0,1,0)\), and
\[
\left.\frac{\partial T(u,C)}{\partial u}\right|_{u=C=0}
=-\frac38\ne0.
\tag{15}
\]

At a conductor point use its two smooth normalization branches,
keeping the common parameter \(\tau=t\) fixed. Let \(c'_j,\zeta'_j\)
be derivatives along \(u-u_j\) under that condition. Since
\(d'_j=4u_j^3\), the common tangent and \(d\) acquire a second
independent normal direction exactly when at least one of
\[
\Delta_c=\frac{c'_j}{4u_j^3}-\frac{c'_k}{4u_k^3},\qquad
\Delta_\zeta=\frac{\zeta'_j}{4u_j^3}
             -\frac{\zeta'_k}{4u_k^3}
\tag{16}
\]
is nonzero. Branch order is that in the pair list of Section 1.
Every \(\Delta_c\) is linear. The following complete check covers
its only possible zero:

| Pair | \(\Delta_c\) | Its zero \(t_0\) | \(\Delta_\zeta(t_0)\) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | \(-i(t-(1+i)/4)\) | \((1+i)/4\) | \(3/16\) |
| 2 | \((-1-2i)(t+1/10+i/20)\) | \(-1/10-i/20\) | \(9381/20000+4691i/40000\) |
| 3 | \((1-2i)(t+1/20+i/10)\) | \(-1/20-i/10\) | \(9381/20000-4691i/40000\) |

All entries in the last column are nonzero. The checker obtains
them from (1), (4), and (6) by implicit differentiation with
\(\tau\) fixed. The completed pinched local ring is
\(\mathbb C[[\xi,\eta,t]]/(\xi\eta)\). Its cotangent space has
exactly the two normal directions and one common tangent used in
(16). Thus the table proves surjectivity onto the entire
three-dimensional cotangent space of \(R\), not merely onto
the two normalization tangent spaces separately.

## 6. Why these checks imply equality

Let \(\mathfrak m\) be any maximal ideal of \(S\). Localize the
finite extension, writing
\[
A_*=S_{\mathfrak m},\qquad B_*=R\otimes_S S_{\mathfrak m}.
\]
The point separation gives exactly one maximal ideal
\(\mathfrak n\) of \(B_*\), so \(B_*\) is local, and its
residue field equals that of \(A_*\). Cotangent surjectivity gives
\[
\mathfrak n=\mathfrak mB_*+\mathfrak n^2.
\]
Apply Nakayama over \(B_*\) to
\(\mathfrak n/\mathfrak mB_*\): then
\(\mathfrak n=\mathfrak mB_*\). The residue-field equality gives
\(B_*=A_*+\mathfrak mB_*\). Apply Nakayama over \(A_*\) to
the finite module \(B_*/A_*\): it is zero. Hence the finite
module \(R/S\) vanishes at every maximal ideal of \(S\), proving
\(R=S\).

Finiteness, residue-field equality, and cotangent surjectivity on
the pinched ring are all used. A set-theoretic bijection or an
immersion of the normalization alone would not justify this step.

## 7. An exact elimination presentation and the remaining gap

There is a concrete finite elimination presentation. Let
\(P=\mathbb C[C,T,Z_0,K,D]\), adjoin \(U_0\), and set
\[
z_*=Z_0+\beta(C,T)U_0,
\]
\[
y_*=U(U_0)(C-a(U_0))+
V(U_0)\left(T-iz_*(C-a(U_0))+\tfrac i2b(U_0)z_*\right).
\]
Let \(J\subset P[U_0]\) be generated by
\[
\begin{split}
&A(U_0)z_*-y_*(y_*-1),\\
&C-a(U_0)-b(U_0)y_*,\\
&T-\tfrac i2b(U_0)(2y_*-1)z_*-p(U_0)y_*,\\
&K-U_0^2+CU_0,\qquad D-U_0^4+1.
\end{split}
\tag{17}
\]
The recovery identity (9) verifies directly that
\(P[U_0]/J\cong B\). Consequently
\[
R\cong P/(J\cap P).
\tag{18}
\]
This is a specified polynomial elimination problem with a prime
elimination ideal, not a computed minimal list of relations in
five variables. No unmentioned saturation is used.

Two rigorous failed triples clarify what remains. No
\(\{d,du,g\}\), for any \(g\in R\), can generate \(R\):
its quotient by \((d,du)\) would be one-generated, but (3)
gives \(\mathbb C[t]^3\). A dimension-one quotient of a
one-variable polynomial ring must be that ring itself, a domain.

Also \(\{c,\tau,\zeta\}\) fails at the actual points
\[
(u,y,z)=(0,0,0),\qquad(1+i,0,0).
\]
Both give \((c,\tau,\zeta)=(m,0,0)\), whereas \(d=-1\)
and \(d=-5\), respectively. They are distinct points of \(R\),
because neither lies on the conductor.

The established bound is
\[
3\le\text{minimum number of complex algebra generators of }R\le5.
\]
The lower bound uses its three-dimensional local cotangent spaces.
There is no universal obstruction here to three or four generators.
Even an eventual embedding into \(\mathbb A^3\) would not supply
the additional ambient LND and quotient compatibility.

proof, not parameter sampling. The geometry and ring-theoretic

required for these explicit formulas.
