# The fiber involution has a unimodular odd row, not an automatic coordinate change

In every constant-acceleration coordinate fiber, the odd coefficients
of any actual polynomial coordinate system form a unimodular row over
the original invariant ring. The involution also supplies exact
Jacobian and first-order identities along its fixed surface.

Neither an associated polynomial vector field nor an invariant-matrix
completion of that row is automatically a coordinate operation. A
fully polynomial sliced example below shows both failures, even though
its involution is itself linearizable. No unconditional recognition
claim follows from these row identities.

## 1. Actual fiber and parity coefficients

Use the actual constant-acceleration reduction of the
[involution note](constant-acceleration-involution-boundary-pass87.md).
Thus E is a sliced locally nilpotent derivation on an actual polynomial
four-ring, z is an original coordinate, and q=E squared z is a nonzero
complex constant. With u=E(z)/q and r=z-qu squared/2, one has
\[
\begin{gathered}
B=A[u]=\mathbb C^{[4]},\quad A=\ker E,\quad E(u)=1,
\quad z=r+(q/2)u^2,\\
C=B/(z-\lambda)\simeq\mathbb C[x_1,x_2,x_3],\quad
C=A\oplus Au,\quad u^2=2(\lambda-r)/q,\\
\iota|_A=\operatorname{id},\quad\iota(u)=-u,
\quad C^\iota=A.
\end{gathered}
\tag{1}
\]
The same letters denote the injected elements of A in the coordinate
fiber. Let x_1,x_2,x_3 be its actual polynomial coordinates, namely
the images of the three original companions of z. Their unique parity
decompositions are
\[
x_i=P_i+uQ_i,\qquad P_i,Q_i\in A,
\qquad \iota(x_i)=x_i-2uQ_i.
\tag{2}
\]
No time-degree bound on the original companions is needed. In this
broader case Q_i is the odd coefficient in the fiber decomposition,
not necessarily the original velocity E(x_i). When an original
companion has E-degree at most one, its coefficient does coincide
with that invariant velocity. These two situations are not identified
without that extra premise.

## 2. The exact coefficient ideal

For any polynomial f in the actual coordinates, telescoping one
coordinate at a time puts iota(f)-f in the ideal generated by the
three coordinate differences. On the other hand, decomposition (1)
makes every difference a multiple of u, and iota(u)-u=-2u. Hence
the full difference ideal is
\[
\bigl(\iota(f)-f:f\in C\bigr)
=(uQ_1,uQ_2,uQ_3)C=(u)C,
\qquad (Q_1,Q_2,Q_3)A=A.
\tag{3}
\]
For the last equality, cancel the nonzero element u in the domain C
to obtain 1=sum c_i Q_i with c_i in C. Taking the even part of each
c_i gives the same identity with coefficients in A. Equivalently,
the free extension A to A direct sum Au is faithfully flat and
contracts the coefficient ideal exactly. This proves a unit ideal,
not merely coprimality of its generators.

This is also an exact divided-difference construction: express u as
a polynomial in x_1,x_2,x_3, telescope u(iota(x))-u(x)=-2u, and
cancel -2u. Averaging the resulting coefficients gives the A-valued
unit-ideal identity in (3). No assumption that u is an ambient
coordinate enters the argument.

## 3. Jacobian and fixed-scheme identities

Write Q as a column, du as the row of ordinary coordinate derivatives,
and JQ for the three-by-three Jacobian of Q in x. Let
V_Q=sum Q_i partial_(x_i) be the associated polynomial derivation.
Direct differentiation of (2) gives
\[
\operatorname{Jac}\iota
=\operatorname{Id}_3-2Q\,du-2u\,JQ,
\qquad
du(Q)\equiv1\pmod{u},\quad (JQ)Q\equiv0\pmod{u}.
\tag{4}
\]
The congruences use ordinary polynomial Taylor expansions and retain
the full quotient C/(u). Indeed, modulo u squared the identity
u(x-2uQ)=-u(x) gives
u-2u du(Q)=-u. Cancelling u yields the first congruence. Applying
the same expansion to each invariant polynomial Q_i gives
-2u times V_Q(Q_i) in (u squared), yielding the second.

The Jacobian determinant is a nonzero complex constant, since iota
is a polynomial automorphism. Reducing (4) modulo u and using the
rank-one determinant identity gives that constant as minus one:
\[
\det\operatorname{Jac}\iota=-1,\qquad
\operatorname{Jac}\iota\equiv\operatorname{Id}_3-2Q\,du\pmod{u},
\qquad (Q\,du)^2\equiv Q\,du\pmod{u}.
\tag{5}
\]
The quotient C/(u) is nonzero: u is nonzero and cannot be a unit
because a unit of C is constant and iota(u)=-u. Thus comparison of
the complex constants modulo (u) is valid. The row du is unimodular
on this quotient by (4), and Q supplies a splitting normal direction.
These identities do not show V_Q(u)=1 globally or that V_Q is locally
nilpotent. In particular they do not produce an A-derivation sending
u to one: such a derivation would contradict the invariant relation
u squared in A in characteristic zero.

## 4. An actual countercontrol to two coordinate upgrades

Start with genuine polynomial coordinates (u,a,b,c) and E=partial_u.
Introduce the actual original coordinates
\[
\begin{gathered}
x=u,\qquad y=(1+u^2)a+ub,\qquad z=ua+b,\qquad W=c+u^2,\\
u=x,\qquad a=y-xz,\qquad
b=(1+x^2)z-xy,\qquad c=W-x^2.
\end{gathered}
\tag{6}
\]
The second line is a polynomial inverse in both orders. The relevant
two-by-two matrix has determinant (1+u squared)-u squared=1.
Thus the transported E is an actual globally sliced LND, and its
original coordinate W has E squared W=2. Here y has E-degree two,
which is allowed by the broader one-coordinate hypothesis.

On W=lambda, the actual three-ring is C=C[u,a,b], with involution
u to -u fixing a and b. The injected original kernel is
A=C[a,b,lambda-u squared]=C[a,b,u squared]. The parity data in the
actual coordinates x,y,z are
\[
P=(0,(1+u^2)a,b)^t,\qquad Q=(1,b,a)^t,
\qquad V_Q(a)=-2xa\ne0.
\tag{7}
\]
For the last identity use a=y-xz, b=z-xa, and
V_Q=partial_x+b partial_y+a partial_z. A locally nilpotent
derivation of a characteristic-zero domain cannot send a nonzero
polynomial into its own nonzero multiple: additivity of its degree
would give deg V_Q(a)=deg a-1 on the one hand and degree at least
deg a on the other. Thus V_Q is not locally nilpotent. This holds
despite the stronger identity V_Q(u)=1 in this example and despite
the first entry of the invariant row Q being one.

There is an equally explicit failure of an invariant-matrix coordinate
completion. In SL_3(A) take
\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\-b&1&0\\-a&0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad MQ=(1,0,0)^t,\qquad
M(x,y,z)^t=(u,(1+u^2)a,b)^t.
\tag{8}
\]
As a change of a free module over A, this matrix is invertible and
does normalize Q. Its displayed polynomial map is not an ambient
automorphism: its Jacobian determinant in the actual coordinates
x,y,z is 1+x squared. This follows either by direct differentiation
or by using (6), whose three-variable Jacobian determinant is one.
The determinant is not a constant unit. The coefficients of M are
themselves functions of x,y,z, so multiplying coordinate columns by
an invertible invariant matrix does not by itself give a coordinate
change.

This is also the standard Reynolds map in the supplied three-variable
frame. The origin is fixed and the derivative of iota there is
L=diag(-1,1,1). Thus (id+L iota)/2 is exactly the map in (8),
and its determinant is 1+x squared. The involution is nevertheless
tame: the actual map from (u,a,b) to (x,y,z) is the composition
z=b+ua followed by y=a+uz, with u fixed, and it conjugates the
linear sign involution. Failure of this particular average therefore
does not imply failure of linearization or of another coordinate
frame. The exact degree-band hypothesis of the primary Reynolds
criterion is audited separately in the
[source addendum](involution-reynolds-source-gate-pass87.md);
no source theorem is required for the countercontrol here.

The involution in this example is already linear in the genuine
coordinates u,a,b. The example therefore refutes neither conditional
linearization nor recognition; it excludes the stated unjustified
LND and coordinate-completion steps. No actual three-good-coordinate
counterexample is claimed, because the original y in (6) is quadratic
in u. The general row proof, like the constant-acceleration fiber
reduction, requires no such additional three-good hypothesis.
