# Intrinsic additive quotients are affine planes

Let \(K\) be a complex affine domain with

\[
K[s]\cong\mathbb C^{[4]},
\]

and let \(0\ne d\in\operatorname{LND}(K)\). Put
\(A=\ker d\), \(X=\operatorname{Spec}K\), and
\(S=\operatorname{Spec}A\). The following conclusions hold:

1. \(A\cong\mathbb C^{[2]}\).
2. The quotient \(\pi:X\to S\) is surjective and faithfully
   flat, with nonempty purely one-dimensional fibers.
3. A nonempty principal open of \(S\) has a trivial
   translation quotient \(\pi^{-1}(S_f)\cong S_f\times\mathbb A^1\).
4. If the \(\mathbb G_a\)-action of \(d\) is free, then
   \(K=A[t]\cong\mathbb C^{[3]}\), and the action is a
   translation in \(t\).

The substantive prior input is Kaliman--Saveliev, not the theorem
about kernels of derivations on a polynomial ring in three
variables. Their result applies to \(X\) itself, whose
polynomiality has not been assumed. The natural inclusion
\(A\subset K[s]\) need not admit an algebra retraction, even
when \(K\) already is polynomial. An explicit example below
also excludes automatic smoothness or connectedness of all quotient
fibers.

## 1. Exact primary inputs

Kaliman and Saveliev's *C+-actions on contractible threefolds*
contains the needed statements for a smooth contractible affine
complex threefold with a nontrivial additive action:[^ks]

- Lemma 2.1(1): the affine quotient has nonempty curve fibers.
- Theorem 2.7: the quotient is a smooth contractible affine surface.
- Corollary 2.8(2): domination of the threefold by a product of a
  curve and \(\mathbb A^2\) makes the quotient \(\mathbb A^2\).
- Corollary 2.8(1),(3): a free action gives a translation product;
  with the preceding domination, the threefold is \(\mathbb A^3\).

The author-hosted final text and the arXiv version were checked.
The authors also give an independent argument for the dominated
case in Section 3. No smoothness or acyclicity of the quotient is
inserted as an unproved premise here.

## 2. The cylinder verifies the domination hypothesis

The cylinder isomorphism gives

\[
X\times\mathbb A^1\cong\mathbb A^4.
\tag{1}
\]

It follows that \(X\) is a smooth affine threefold. One can see
smoothness directly from the local embedding-dimension formula
for adjoining a variable. Topologically, \(X\) is a deformation
retract of its product with \(\mathbb C\), so it is contractible.

Transporting the first projection in (1) gives a smooth surjective
morphism

\[
P:\mathbb A^4\longrightarrow X
\]

with differential of rank three everywhere. Choose a point \(v\)
and a three-dimensional vector subspace complementary to the
one-dimensional kernel of \(dP_v\). Its translate through \(v\)
is an affine linear three-plane \(L\cong\mathbb A^3\). The
restriction \(P|_L\) has full differential rank at \(v\), hence
is dominant. Thus \(X\) is dominated by

\[
L\cong\mathbb A^1\times\mathbb A^2,
\]

exactly the source required by Corollary 2.8(2). No birationality,
global section, or surjectivity of this restriction is needed.

Applying that corollary to every nonzero \(d\) proves

\[
\boxed{A\cong\mathbb C^{[2]}.}
\tag{2}
\]

This does not treat the original \(s\) as an ambient polynomial
coordinate. Its role is only to supply (1).

### Why the surface-classification hypotheses matter

Factoriality, normality, and constant units of \(A\) were already
available from factorial closure and the cylinder. They alone
should not be substituted for a smooth-surface classification
theorem. In this argument, the published quotient theorem supplies
the missing geometry.

There is a compatible alternative once Theorem 2.7 has established
smoothness. The inclusion \(A\subset\mathbb C^{[4]}\) gives
a dominant morphism from affine space to the smooth factorial
surface \(S\). Miyanishi's regular-subring result then identifies
it with \(\mathbb A^2\); the exact regular, two-dimensional,
factorial hypothesis is restated in the introduction of his
*Regular subrings of a polynomial ring, II*.[^miy]
This is not an inference from rationality alone. The direct
Kaliman--Saveliev corollary already proves (2), so no additional
classification assertion is needed for the main route.

## 3. Surjectivity and flatness

Surjectivity does not follow merely from finite generation of
additive invariants. Here it follows from the specific nonempty
fiber statement in Kaliman--Saveliev Lemma 2.1(1). Together with
(2), write \(A=\mathbb C[a,b]\).

For a closed point \(p=(\alpha,\beta)\in S\), its fiber is
defined in the smooth threefold \(X\) by
\((a-\alpha,b-\beta)K\). It is nonempty and has dimension
one by the cited lemma. Its components are all one-dimensional:
an ideal generated by two elements in a regular threefold cannot
have a minimal component of codimension three, while a surface
component would contradict the stated curve dimension. The two
equations consequently form a regular sequence locally along
the fiber, so embedded components do not introduce a separate
dimension problem.

At a closed point \(x\in\pi^{-1}(p)\), let

\[
R=\mathcal O_{S,p},\qquad T=\mathcal O_{X,x}.
\]

The ring \(R\) is regular of dimension two, \(T\) is regular
and hence Cohen--Macaulay of dimension three, and
\(\dim(T/\mathfrak m_R T)=1\). Thus

\[
\dim T=\dim R+\dim(T/\mathfrak m_R T).
\]

Miracle flatness gives flatness at \(x\).[^flat] The flat locus
is open for this morphism of finite presentation over a noetherian
base; since it contains every closed point of the affine
\(\mathbb C\)-variety \(X\), it is all of \(X\).
Surjectivity therefore gives

\[
\boxed{A\longrightarrow K\text{ is faithfully flat}.}
\tag{3}
\]

Neither reducedness nor smoothness of every fiber is required by
this argument. Both would be stronger conclusions, and the
example in Section 5 disproves them in general.

## 4. Local slices and the remaining global condition

Choose a local slice \(r\in K\) with
\(d^2(r)=0\) and \(f=d(r)\ne0\). Then \(f\in A\),
and in \(K_f\), the element \(t=r/f\) satisfies \(d(t)=1\).
The local slice theorem gives

\[
K_f=A_f[t],\qquad d=\partial_t.
\tag{4}
\]

For completeness, the finite expression
\(\sum_j(-t)^j d^j(k)/j!\) is invariant for every
\(k\in K_f\); the resulting Taylor expansion writes \(k\)
as a polynomial in \(t\) over \(A_f\). This proves the
local polynomial statement without assuming a global slice.

If the action is free, Corollary 2.8(1) gives the global translation
product \(X\cong S\times\mathbb A^1\), and (2) then gives
\(K\cong\mathbb C^{[3]}\). Over \(\mathbb C\), an
additive-group stabilizer is either trivial or the whole group,
so freeness is equivalent to absence of fixed points. In algebraic
terms, this says that the ideal of \(K\) generated by \(d(K)\)
is the unit ideal.

Thus any hypothetical exotic \(K\) satisfying (1) would have
fixed points for every nonzero intrinsic additive action. This is
stronger than only recording failure of properness or of a slice.
It does not prove that a free intrinsic action exists.

Extending \(d\) by \(d(s)=0\) gives

\[
\ker\widetilde d=A[s]\cong\mathbb C^{[3]}.
\]

Its quotient is the product of \(\pi\) with the identity on
\(\mathbb A^1_s\), hence is also faithfully flat. This is an
abstract polynomial-kernel conclusion. It does not exhibit an
ambient coordinate of \(\mathbb C^{[4]}\) in that kernel.

## 5. Exact counterexample to a natural-inclusion retraction

Take

\[
K=\mathbb C[x,y,z],\qquad
d=x\partial_y+2y\partial_z,
\qquad v=xz-y^2.
\tag{5}
\]

This is a nonzero triangular LND, with \(d(x)=d(v)=0\),
and

\[
A=\ker d=\mathbb C[x,v].
\tag{6}
\]

Here is a direct kernel verification. After inverting \(x\),
\(K_x=\mathbb C[x,x^{-1},v,y]\) and \(d=x\partial_y\).
An invariant polynomial in \(K\) can therefore be written
\(x^{-m}Q(x,v)\). If \(m>0\) and \(Q\) has no factor
\(x\), polynomiality in \(K\) would imply
\(Q(0,-y^2)=0\). Substitution \(\mathbb C[v]\to\mathbb C[y]\),
\(v\mapsto-y^2\), is injective, so this is impossible.
Removing powers of \(x\) proves (6).

Suppose an algebra retraction \(\varphi:K\to A\) of the
given inclusion existed. Put \(Y=\varphi(y)\) and
\(Z=\varphi(z)\), polynomials in \(x,v\). Since
\(\varphi\) fixes \(x,v\), it would satisfy

\[
xZ-Y^2=v.
\]

Reduction modulo \(x\) gives
\(-Y(0,v)^2=v\), which is impossible in \(\mathbb C[v]\).
Thus the natural inclusion has no retraction. Nor does
\(A\subset K[s]\): restricting such a retraction to \(K\)
would give the one just excluded.

Abstractly \(A\) is polynomial and hence is a retract of a
polynomial ring under a different, standard inclusion. The false
assertion is that its actual invariant inclusion here splits.

The fibers of \(\pi(x,y,z)=(x,xz-y^2)\) also show exactly
what faithful flatness permits. For \(x\ne0\) the fiber is
\(\mathbb A^1\). At \(x=0,v=\beta\ne0\), it is two
disjoint affine lines. At \((x,v)=(0,0)\), its ring is
\(\mathbb C[y,z]/(y^2)\), a double affine line. The fixed
locus of the action is \(\{x=y=0\}\).

This example meets the exact cylinder hypotheses with
\(K[s]=\mathbb C^{[4]}\). It disproves automatic retraction,
globally trivial affine-line quotient, and freeness, but is not
a cancellation counterexample.

## 6. Scope

The surface part is settled by the cited prior theorem: every
intrinsic nonzero LND has affine-plane quotient, and the quotient
map is faithfully flat and surjective. Its acyclicity is supported
by a specific theorem, not assumed to descend from the total space.
The remaining structural question is how to obtain a free action
or another condition controlling the exceptional quotient fibers.
The cylinder, factoriality, and local slice alone do not supply it.
