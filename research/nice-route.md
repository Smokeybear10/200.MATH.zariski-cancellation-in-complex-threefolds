# Nice slice derivations and dimension-four quasi-translations

## Scope and outcome

Let

\[
B=\mathbb C[x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4],\qquad
D=\sum_{i=1}^4F_i\partial_{x_i},
\]

and assume that `D` is **nice in the displayed coordinates**, meaning
`D^2(x_i)=0` for every `i`, and that it has a slice `s`, meaning `D(s)=1`.
This is a particularly rigid subproblem of affine three-space cancellation.

The bounded audit did **not** locate a theorem saying that every such
dimension-four derivation fixes an ambient coordinate. It did locate sharp
closures of all homogeneous cases, all cases that already fix a coordinate,
and several low-complexity families. Consequently, the unresolved class left
by the audited results is much smaller:

* `F` must be nonhomogeneous;
* `D` must have full locally-nilpotent-derivation rank four;
* absence of a linear invariant alone is not enough;
* a quadratic `F` can survive the closures below only at generic Jacobian rank
  three, and no slice of degree at most two can be hard.

This is a reduction and a search design, not a solution or novelty claim. No
explicit full-rank nice slice derivation in four variables was found.

## Exact algebra forced by niceness and a slice

Write `F=(F_1,...,F_4)^T`. Then

\[
D^2(x_i)=D(F_i),
\]

so niceness is exactly

\[
D(F)=JF\,F=0. \tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) implies the polynomial identities

\[
F(x+tF(x))=F(x),\qquad
(x+tF)^{-1}=x-tF. \tag{2}
\]

Thus `x+tF` is a quasi-translation and `D` is locally nilpotent: on every
polynomial, `exp(tD)` is substitution by `x+tF`. Conversely, differentiating
the first identity in (2) at `t=0` recovers (1). This is the standard
nice-derivation/quasi-translation correspondence.[^debondt-qt]

Since `x+tF` is a polynomial automorphism,

\[
\det(I+tJF)=1.
\]

It follows that `JF` is nilpotent over `B`. In particular its generic rank is
at most three. This matrix rank is not the rank of the locally nilpotent
derivation; keeping those two notions separate is essential below.

The slice equation is

\[
1=D(s)=\nabla s\cdot F. \tag{3}
\]

Therefore `(F_1,F_2,F_3,F_4)=B`: the vector field has no zero. In fact the
components are unimodular already over the invariant ring `A=ker D`. Apply the
slice projection

\[
\pi(q)=\sum_{j\geq0}\frac{(-s)^j}{j!}D^j(q)
\]

to (3). Because every `F_i` is invariant,

\[
1=\sum_i \pi(\partial_i s)F_i,
\qquad \pi(\partial_i s)\in A. \tag{4}
\]

The converse is false in general: a unimodular coefficient vector says that
the additive action is fixed-point-free, not that `1` lies in the image of
`D`. Bhatwadekar--Gupta--Lokhande construct a fixed-point-free locally
nilpotent derivation on a four-variable polynomial ring whose kernel is
singular, so it cannot have a slice. Thus a coefficient search must impose the
actual equation `D(s)=1`, not merely the unit-ideal condition.[^bgl]

For the coordinate generators, niceness truncates this projection after one
term:

\[
G_i:=\pi(x_i)=x_i-sF_i. \tag{5}
\]

The slice theorem gives

\[
B=A[s],\qquad A=\mathbb C[G_1,G_2,G_3,G_4]. \tag{6}
\]

There must be algebraic relations among the four `G_i`, because `A` has
transcendence degree three. Formula (5) is nevertheless useful: it makes the
quotient a polynomially embedded retract without deciding whether it is
affine three-space.

Indeed, set `S=V(s)`. Equations (2)--(5) give

\[
s(G(x))=0,\quad F(G(x))=F(x),\quad G(G(x))=G(x),
\]

and the maps

\[
S\times\mathbb A^1\longrightarrow\mathbb A^4,
\quad(q,t)\longmapsto q+tF(q),
\]

and `x -> (G(x),s(x))` are inverse. The hypersurface `S` is smooth because
`\nabla s` cannot vanish by (3). Thus the slice already recovers the original
cancellation configuration `S x A^1 ~= A^4`; niceness adds the fact that all
orbits are literal affine lines with direction constant along each orbit. It
does not by itself identify `S` with `A^3`.

Differentiating (5) gives

\[
JG=I-F(\nabla s)^T-sJF,\qquad JG\,F=0. \tag{7}
\]

On `S`, the first expression becomes the rank-three projection
`I-F(\nabla s)^T`. If `JF` has generic rank three, then over the fraction field
its kernel is generated by `F`; (4) upgrades this to the exact module identity

\[
\ker_B(JF)=BF. \tag{8}
\]

To see this, choose the invariant row `q` in (4), so `qF=1`. If `JFv=0`, then
`v=hF` over `Frac(B)` and hence `h=qv` lies in `B`. Geometrically, on the
rank-three locus the restriction `F|_S` has injective differential: the kernel
of `dF` is the orbit direction `F`, while `F` is transverse to `T S` by (3).
Thus `F|_S` is generically etale onto its three-dimensional image. Generic
etaleness does not imply global injectivity, properness, or that `S` is affine
space, so this observation does not close the problem.

There is also an exact rank-three matrix identity useful for elimination. Put
`N=JF`. Since the characteristic polynomial of `N` is `T^4`,

\[
\operatorname{adj}(N)=-N^3.
\]

Every column of `N^3` lies in `ker N=BF`. If `qF=1` is the invariant Bezout row
from (4), then

\[
N^3=F L,\qquad L=qN^3,\qquad LN=0,\qquad LF=0. \tag{8a}
\]

Moreover, `trdeg C(F)=3`, so the prime ideal of relations among the four
components is generated by an irreducible polynomial `P`, and
\((\nabla P)(F)\) spans the generic left kernel of `N`. Thus `L` and
\((\nabla P)(F)\) are proportional over `Frac(B)`. This converts both left and
right null directions into explicit polynomial data, but controlling the
proportionality factor or proving that `P` is a coordinate remains an open
step in this route.

## Three different ranks and the first decisive closure

The following quantities must not be conflated.

1. `rank(JF)` is the generic matrix rank, equivalently the transcendence
   degree of the components in the relevant separable setting.
2. The complex linear span of the component functions measures whether there
   is a **linear** invariant.
3. `rank(D)` is the minimum number of nonfixed variables after arbitrary
   polynomial changes of coordinates.

If a nonzero constant row `lambda` satisfies

\[
\lambda F=0,
\]

then `lambda x` is an invariant linear ambient coordinate. More generally, if
`lambda F=c` is a nonzero constant, then `(lambda x)/c` is a linear coordinate
slice. Either condition settles cancellation in this setting.

There is a stronger nonlinear version. If a slice locally nilpotent derivation
on `C^[4]` fixes **any** ambient coordinate `u`, regard it as a derivation of
`C[u]^[3]`. The dimension-three-over-a-Dedekind-domain theorem of Derksen,
van den Essen, and van Rossum makes its kernel `C[u]^[2]`; adjoining the slice
then conjugates `D` to a coordinate partial derivative.[^derksen] Equivalently,
for a slice derivation in dimension four,

\[
\operatorname{rank}(D)<4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{rank}(D)=1. \tag{9}
\]

The same closure is compatible with Bhatwadekar--Gupta--Lokhande: a slice
makes the kernel regular, and their invariant-coordinate theorem makes that
kernel polynomial.[^bgl]

Therefore a negative example inside the nice-slice class would have to have
full LND rank four. Conversely, if `ker D` were a polynomial ring, (6) would
make its three generators together with `s` a coordinate system and `D` would
have rank one. In this slice setting, a genuine rank-four example would already
be a cancellation counterexample, not merely an exotic presentation of a
known translation.

## What the established quasi-translation results remove

### Homogeneous maps in four variables

De Bondt proves that a homogeneous quasi-translation in dimension at most four
has image span of dimension at most two; Corollary 5.9 gives, after a linear
change, the more explicit form

\[
F=(0,0,bg,ag),
\]

with `a,b` in two variables and `g` invariant in the stated normal form.[^debondt-qt]
There are therefore at least two independent linear invariants. A purely
homogeneous positive-degree `F` could not have a slice anyway, because all its
components vanish at the origin. The classification is still important: any
full-rank survivor must be genuinely nonhomogeneous, and homogenizing it does
not simply return a dimension-four case.

Wang's three-variable theorem is stronger in lower dimension: a nonzero nice
derivation on `k^[3]` has a nonzero linear invariant and, in the irreducible
case, rank one with an explicit normal form.[^wang] Dasgupta--Gupta extend
related results over PIDs and prove the four-variable conclusion when a fixed
coordinate/rank-at-most-three hypothesis is already present.[^dasgupta]
Their abstract must not be read as proving that every nice derivation in four
variables has rank at most three.

### No linear invariant is not enough

Nonhomogeneous quasi-translations in four variables can have no invariant
linear form. De Bondt's Example 4.3 supplies such a polynomially conjugated
example.[^debondt-qt] That construction does not have a slice: its original
coefficient vector has a common factor and hence fixed points.

More pointedly, dehomogenizing de Bondt's dimension-five Example 1.3(b) gives
the four-variable vector

\[
F=(1,b,b^2,-b^2x_1+2bx_2-x_3),
\qquad b=x_1x_3-x_2^2+x_4. \tag{10}
\]

It has the slice `x_1` and no linear invariant, but `b` is an invariant ambient
coordinate. In coordinates

\[
(x_1,b,x_2-x_1b,x_3-x_1b^2)
\]

the derivation is a translation.[^debondt-5] Thus a search that merely forces
the absence of linear invariants will rediscover solved rank-one actions. Every
candidate must be tested for arbitrary polynomial invariant coordinates or,
more operationally, for LND rank four.

## Two elementary closed subfamilies

### A constant plus one homogeneous layer

Suppose

\[
F=c+H_d,
\]

where `c` is constant and `H_d` is homogeneous of degree `d>=2`. Separating
degrees in `JF F=0` yields

\[
JH_d H_d=0,\qquad JH_d c=0. \tag{11}
\]

Let `W` be the linear span of the image of `H_d`. The homogeneous
dimension-four theorem gives `dim W<=2`. If `c` is not in `W`, choose a linear
functional `lambda` vanishing on `W` with `lambda(c)=1`; then `lambda F=1`, so
`lambda x` is a coordinate slice. If `c` lies in `W`, choose a nonzero
`lambda` annihilating `W`; then `lambda F=0`, so `lambda x` is an invariant
coordinate. This entire two-layer family therefore closes by established
results plus linear algebra. The affine-linear case is elementary and is
handled separately in the main project note.

### Quadratic maps of Jacobian rank at most two

For a polynomial map of degree at most two, de Bondt proves that a nilpotent
Jacobian of rank at most two is linearly triangularizable; the same holds when
the Jacobian squares to zero.[^debondt-rank2] Applied here, strict triangularity
means an extreme component of `F` is constant. If that constant is zero, the
corresponding linear variable is invariant; if it is nonzero, that variable
is a coordinate slice. Hence a quadratic full-rank candidate must satisfy

\[
\operatorname{rank}(JF)=3. \tag{12}
\]

Strong-nilpotence results for quadratic **homogeneous** maps do not remove this
case. For a nonhomogeneous quadratic vector, `JF` has both constant and linear
parts; triangularizing the top homogeneous Jacobian does not prove that the
constant part is triangular in the same basis.[^strong]

## Low-degree slices close by elementary linear algebra

There is a further closure independent of niceness and of the degree of `F`.

**Lemma.** A polynomial `s` of total degree at most two on affine space over
`C` is a coordinate whenever its gradient has no zero.

**Proof.** Write

\[
s=c+\ell^Tx+\tfrac12x^TSx,
\]

with `S` symmetric. If `ell` belonged to `im S`, the linear system
`Sx=-ell` would have a solution, giving a critical point. Hence
`ell notin im S`. Because the annihilator of `im S` is `ker S`, there is a
vector `v in ker S` with `ell^T v != 0`. Choose a linear coordinate `y_1` in
the direction `v` and complementary linear coordinates. Then

\[
s=(\ell^Tv)y_1+g(y_2,\ldots,y_n),
\]

so replacing `y_1` by `s` has the displayed polynomial inverse
`y_1=(s-g)/(ell^Tv)`. ∎

For a slice, `D(s)=1` prevents `grad s` from vanishing. Consequently every
slice of degree at most two is already an ambient coordinate and settles the
cancellation question. In particular, there is no hard nice-slice family with
a quadratic (or linear) displayed slice.

## The first finite quadratic search packet not already closed

Take `F` quadratic and write

\[
F=F_0+F_1+F_2,
\]

where `F_j` is homogeneous of degree `j`. The nice equation is equivalent,
degree by degree, to

\[
\begin{aligned}
JF_2F_2&=0,\\
JF_2F_1+JF_1F_2&=0,\\
JF_2F_0+JF_1F_1&=0,\\
JF_1F_0&=0. \tag{13}
\end{aligned}
\]

Unimodularity forces `F_0 != 0`, since otherwise the origin would be a common
zero. By the preceding lemma, the first slice-degree packet not already closed
must allow a cubic term:

\[
s=s_0+s_1+s_2+s_3.
\]

Equating degrees in `Ds=1` gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla s_3\cdot F_2&=0,\\
\nabla s_3\cdot F_1+\nabla s_2\cdot F_2&=0,\\
\nabla s_3\cdot F_0+\nabla s_2\cdot F_1+\nabla s_1\cdot F_2&=0,\\
\nabla s_2\cdot F_0+\nabla s_1\cdot F_1&=0,\\
\nabla s_1\cdot F_0&=1. \tag{14}
\end{aligned}
\]

A linear change of variables can normalize simultaneously

\[
F_0=e_4,\qquad s_1=x_4. \tag{15}
\]

Then the last equation of (13) says that the fourth column of `JF_1` is zero,
and the degree-one equation in (14) becomes

\[
\partial_{x_4}s_2+(F_1)_4=0. \tag{16}
\]

Equations (13)--(16), together with nonvanishing of a `3 x 3` minor of `JF`,
are a finite coefficient system for a quadratic nice derivation with a cubic
slice. A defensible computational program is:

1. quotient the coefficient scheme by the residual linear transformations
   preserving (15);
2. saturate by the rank-three minor and by conditions excluding affine-linear
   invariants `lambda F in C`;
3. solve the analogous linear coefficient system for a slice of degree at most
   two and discard every point where it is consistent;
4. for each remaining component, attempt to exhibit a polynomial invariant
   coordinate or prove that the component is empty;
5. independently verify every proposed family by exact substitution in
   (13)--(16), and separately establish local nilpotence/rank rather than
   inferring it from numerical samples.

This packet is genuinely bounded, but it is not known from this audit to
contain a survivor. Nor does it cover a quadratic nice derivation whose
minimal slice has degree greater than three. Calling it an “open bounded-degree
family” would therefore be too strong; it is the first explicit coefficient
search region not eliminated by the preceding arguments.

Homogenization also needs care. Homogenizing a nonhomogeneous four-variable
quasi-translation produces a homogeneous map in five variables with a built-in
zero component, hence an automatic linear invariant. Results about homogeneous
dimension-five maps having no linear invariant cannot be applied to demand a
second invariant or an invariant coordinate in the dehomogenized action.

## Claim-status recommendation

The safe statement is:

> The located primary results do not settle all nonhomogeneous nice locally
> nilpotent derivations with a slice on `C^[4]`. They do settle the homogeneous,
> invariant-coordinate, constant-plus-one-homogeneous-layer, and quadratic
> Jacobian-rank-at-most-two cases, as well as every case admitting a slice of
> degree at most two. Any remaining quadratic candidate must have `rank(JF)=3`
> and full LND rank four; the first finite packet to test permits a cubic slice.

This should remain **research reduction / candidate search**, not “plausibly
novel theorem.” A recent preprint states the related linearization question as
open in dimensions at least four, but its first version contains a sign error
in a displayed slice change and a purported Makar--Limanov obstruction that is
vacuous for a ring already assumed polynomial. It is useful as a literature
locator, not as sole authority for status or proof.[^cid]
