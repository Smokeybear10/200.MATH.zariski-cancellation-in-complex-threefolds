# An explicit polynomial frame for a unimodular quadric velocity

A rank-one two-by-two polynomial matrix whose four entries generate
the unit ideal admits a global Segre frame. This elementary construction
uses gcd division and two Bezout identities. It does not require a
projective-module completion theorem or invert a polynomial on the base.

The application below is conditional on the actual quadric-velocity
hypotheses. It does not produce a nice frame for an arbitrary action.

## 1. Factorization and completion over the actual base

Let S be a UFD, and let the nonzero matrix
\[
\mathcal P=\begin{pmatrix}P_1&P_2\\P_3&P_4\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \det\mathcal P=0,
\qquad (P_1,P_2,P_3,P_4)S=S.
\tag{1}
\]
Then there are columns p,q in S squared such that
\[
\mathcal P=pq^t,
\qquad (p_1,p_2)S=(q_1,q_2)S=S.
\tag{2}
\]
To prove this, choose a nonzero column and divide its coefficient gcd
to obtain a primitive column p. Every other column is a rational
multiple of p because the matrix has rank one over Frac(S). A reduced
denominator of that multiplier divides both entries of p, so is a unit.
The multipliers are therefore in S and give q. The ideal in (1) is
the product of the two coefficient ideals in (2). A product equal to S
forces each factor ideal to equal S. A zero entry or zero column causes
no problem; only the initial selected column must be nonzero.

Choose p' and q' using the two Bezout identities so that
\[
L=[p,p']\in\operatorname{SL}_2(S),\qquad
K=[q,q']\in\operatorname{SL}_2(S).
\tag{3}
\]
For example, if a p_1+b p_2=1, take p'=(-b,a)^t.
Flatten matrices in row-major order and define the four columns
\[
P=pq^t,\quad V=p(q')^t,\quad W=p'q^t,\quad U=p'(q')^t.
\qquad M=[P,V,W,U]\in\operatorname{SL}_4(S).
\tag{4}
\]
Indeed the map represented by M is C mapped to L C K^t on two-by-two
matrices. Its determinant is (det L) squared times (det K) squared,
which is one. Its inverse is explicitly C mapped to L inverse C
K inverse-transpose, and has polynomial coefficients in S.

Use the polar form of the determinant,
\[
\langle X,Z\rangle=X_1Z_4+X_4Z_1-X_2Z_3-X_3Z_2.
\tag{5}
\]
Both left and right determinant-one changes preserve it. Thus in the
ordered frame (P,V,W,U) its only nonzero pairings are
\[
\langle P,U\rangle=1,\qquad \langle V,W\rangle=-1,
\tag{6}
\]
and the symmetric counterparts. In particular the covector
n equal to the gradient of det at P is exactly the fourth row of
M inverse. It is unimodular and annihilates P,V,W.

## 2. Actual coordinates and the relative relation

Now take the actual setting of
[common-top79](nice-common-top-normal-identity-pass79.md):
B=C[x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4], A=ker E, an original E-slice, a commuting
highest LND R, and S=A intersect ker R=C[u,v]. Suppose further that,
after a constant coordinate change, the velocity P satisfies (1).
The common-top result gives the required unit ideal in S.

Assume rank[P,P_u,P_v]=3. The row n annihilates these three columns
by differentiating det P=0. Since R(x) is in their span and R kills S,
the actual polynomial c=n dot x is killed by E and R. Hence c is in S.
Applying the polynomial matrix M inverse to the original coordinate
column gives actual elements sigma,Y,Z,c of B, with
\[
x=P\sigma+VY+WZ+Uc,\qquad
E\sigma=1,\quad EY=EZ=0,\quad c\in S.
\tag{7}
\]
Both directions of this equality are polynomial over S. Consequently
B=S[sigma,Y,Z]=A[sigma], and taking the E-kernel gives A=S[Y,Z].
The substitution S[y,z] onto A has a height-one prime kernel, by
transcendence degrees. The polynomial ring S[y,z] is a UFD, so this
kernel is generated by one irreducible polynomial f. There is no
claim that sigma,Y,Z,u,v are five independent coordinates of B.

## 3. The transverse linear calculation

Write, for i=u,v,
\[
P_i=\alpha_iP+b_iV+c_iW,
\qquad \Delta=b_uc_v-b_vc_u\ne0.
\tag{8}
\]
There is no U term since n dot P_i=0; all coefficients lie in S.
The nonzero determinant follows from the assumed rank three.
Differentiate n dot V=n dot W=0 and use (5)--(6). The fourth-row
coefficient of the formal derivative of the right side of (7) is
\[
\ell_i=c_iY+b_iZ+d_i,\qquad d_i=n\cdot(Uc)_i\in S.
\tag{9}
\]
In particular it has no sigma term. The affine field obtained from
these coefficients has a diagonal linear part already over Frac(S):
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathscr L={}&(b_u\ell_v-b_v\ell_u)\partial_Y
 +(c_u\ell_v-c_v\ell_u)\partial_Z\\
={}&(\Delta Y+b_ud_v-b_vd_u)\partial_Y
 +(-\Delta Z+c_ud_v-c_vd_u)\partial_Z.
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
\]
Equation (10) is an explicit coefficient identity. A generation
determinant argument is still needed to conclude that the actual
relation f is a Darboux polynomial for this field. The frame alone
does not imply that conclusion or recognize A.

All divisions in Sections 1--2 are justified UFD divisions yielding
actual polynomials. The optional affine translation that removes
the constant terms in (10) takes place only over Frac(S), not on S.
No numerical check, formal proof certification or novelty claim is
part of this note.
