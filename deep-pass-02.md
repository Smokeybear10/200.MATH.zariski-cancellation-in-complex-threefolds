# Slice obstructions in nonlinear cancellation candidates

## Main conclusions

The complex three-dimensional Zariski cancellation problem is not solved here. The question is whether an isomorphism

\[
X\times_{\mathbb C}\mathbb A^1\simeq\mathbb A^4
\]

forces \(X\simeq\mathbb A^3\). The conclusion \(\mathbb A^1\) in the original statement must be corrected unless the intended dimension is one. The choice of complex threefolds remains a working interpretation, not a clarification from the proposer.

There is, however, a complete answer for two explicit families of nonlinear actions that might otherwise look like cancellation candidates. Let

\[
f=xz-y^2,\qquad g=zf^2+2x^2yf+x^5,
\qquad r=-x^3-fy,
\]

and let \(\Delta=\operatorname{Jac}(f,g,\cdot)\) on \(k[x,y,z]\). This is Freudenburg's rank-three derivation, discussed in Section 5 of Dasgupta and Gaifullin.[^1] On \(B=k[x,y,z,w]\), set

\[
D_a=\Delta+a\partial_w.
\]

Throughout these family statements, \(k\) is any field of characteristic zero. A *slice* means a polynomial \(s\in B\) satisfying \(D_a(s)=1\).

| Coefficient family | Exact criterion for a slice | Positive case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| \(a\in k[x,f]\) | \(a\) is a nonzero constant | \(s=w/a\) |
| \(a\in k[f,g]\) | \(a=c+fgP(f,g)\), \(c\in k^\times\) | \(s=(w-Pr)/c\) |

In both families the action is fixed-point-free exactly when \(a(0,0)\ne0\), where the arguments are those of its displayed two-variable coefficient ring. Consequently there are whole families of fixed-point-free actions with no slice. Every positive case in the table has a polynomial kernel, with an explicit polynomial change of coordinates.

These are family-specific propositions with proofs below. Their originality has not been established. The underlying rank-three derivation, local-slice techniques, and the distinction between free actions and translations are established subjects. Neither a publication claim nor a solution of the general cancellation problem follows from the table.

## Why the slice question matters

For an affine cylinder \(X\times\mathbb A^1\), translation in the last factor induces a locally nilpotent derivation with a slice. Conversely, in characteristic zero the slice theorem gives

\[
B=(\ker D)[s]\quad\text{when }D(s)=1.
\]

Thus a cancellation counterexample represented in this way requires a slice derivation on a polynomial ring whose kernel is not polynomial. A free action without a slice does not supply such a representation. Even the existence of a slice does not, by itself, identify its kernel as a polynomial ring.[^1]

An important known positive result settles the case where the derivation fixes an ambient polynomial coordinate and its kernel is regular. A slice provides the regularity hypothesis in the present polynomial setting. Therefore merely exhibiting a fixed coordinate reduces the problem to an existing theorem, not a new general solution.[^2] The use of a rank-three derivation in the smaller ring avoids beginning with a trivially fixed coordinate there; it does not prove that its four-variable extension has rank four.

## Algebraic identities and local nilpotence

Direct calculation gives

\[
\Delta x=2fr,\qquad
\Delta y=-g-6x^2r,
\qquad
\Delta z=2xf^2-10xyr,
\tag{1}
\]

and

\[
\Delta f=\Delta g=0,\qquad
\Delta r=fg,\qquad r^2+f^3=xg.
\tag{2}
\]

There is a short independent proof of local nilpotence, so this part need not rest on a classification theorem. After inverting \(fg\), the original generators have the expressions

\[
x=\frac{r^2+f^3}{g},\qquad
y=-\frac{r+x^3}{f},\qquad
z=\frac{g+2rx^2+x^5}{f^2}.
\tag{3}
\]

The localized ring is generated by invariants and \(r\), and \(\Delta r=fg\) is invariant. Repeated application of \(\Delta\) therefore terminates on every polynomial in this ring. The original polynomial ring embeds in it, so the same is true there. The nilpotence indices of \(x,y,z\) are respectively \(3,7,11\).

For any \(a\in k[x,y,z]\), extending by \(D_aw=a\) remains locally nilpotent: on the old variables it is \(\Delta\), and \(D_a^{n+1}w=\Delta^n a\). Termination on finitely many algebra generators implies termination on their polynomial algebra by the iterated Leibniz rule. This observation is broader than the two coefficient classifications, but does not decide their slice question.

The zero set of \(\Delta\) is exactly \(x=y=0\). To see this, if \(f=0\), then \(\Delta y=5x^5\) forces \(x=0\), and \(f=0\) forces \(y=0\). If \(f\ne0\), the equation \(\Delta x=0\) first gives \(r=0\), then \(\Delta z=0\) gives \(x=0\), and \(r=-x^3-fy\) gives \(y=0\), a contradiction. The converse follows directly from (1).

On this line, both \(f\) and \(g\) vanish. In either coefficient family, \(D_aw\) restricts to \(a(0,0)\). This proves the stated fixed-point-free criterion over an algebraic closure. If \(a(0,0)=0\), evaluation at a fixed point immediately rules out \(D_as=1\).

## An orbit certificate that excludes every polynomial degree

The central calculation has a simple algebraic form. Let \(e,q\ne0\), and consider the point and time

\[
P_e=(0,e,qe^2,0),\qquad T=\frac{2}{qe^5}.
\tag{4}
\]

At \(P_e\),

\[
f=-e^2,\qquad r=e^3,\qquad g=qe^6.
\]

By (2), along the \(\Delta\)-orbit,

\[
r(t)=r+fgt,\qquad
x(t)=x+2frt+f^2gt^2.
\tag{5}
\]

Writing \(t=uT\), the latter becomes

\[
x(uT)=\frac4q(u^2-u).
\tag{6}
\]

At \(t=T\), equations (5) give \(x=0\) and \(r=-e^3\). The defining relations then force \(y=-e\) and \(z=qe^2\). Thus the first three coordinates travel from \((0,e,qe^2)\) to \((0,-e,qe^2)\).

For a coefficient \(a=A(x,f)\), the fourth-coordinate displacement is

\[
w(T)-w(0)
=T\int_0^1 A\left(\frac4q(u^2-u),-e^2\right)\,du.
\tag{7}
\]

This integral is purely algebraic: the linear operation on polynomials given by \(u^n\mapsto1/(n+1)\). No analytic integration over the ground field is required.

Suppose the integral is zero. Then the entire orbit joins the two points

\[
P_e=(0,e,qe^2,0),\qquad Q_e=(0,-e,qe^2,0).
\tag{8}
\]

If a polynomial slice \(s\) existed, its exponential identity would give

\[
s(Q_e)-s(P_e)=T.
\tag{9}
\]

For constant \(q\), the left side lies in \(k[e]\), whereas the right side is \(2/(qe^5)\). This is impossible in \(k[e,e^{-1}]\). Equivalently, after multiplication by \(e^5\), evaluation at \(e=0\) would give \(0=2/q\).

For example, \(A(x,f)=1+x\) permits \(q=2/3\), so \(T=3/e^5\). The fourth-coordinate increment is exactly

\[
\frac{3-9+6}{e^5}=0.
\]

The contradiction in (9) excludes all degrees at once. It is stronger than finding no slice in a large finite-dimensional ansatz. It also provides a concrete reason why this free action cannot be a product translation.

## Classification for coefficients in \(k[x,f]\)

### The elementary one-variable case

Write \(a(x)=\sum_{j=0}^d a_jx^j\), with \(d>0\) and \(a_0\ne0\). Define

\[
I(h)=\sum_{j=0}^d a_j\beta_jh^j,
\qquad
\beta_j=\frac{(-1)^j(j!)^2}{(2j+1)!}.
\tag{10}
\]

The identity

\[
\int_0^1(u^2-u)^j\,du=\beta_j
\]

follows by polynomial integration by parts: for nonnegative integers \(p,q\), successively transferring derivatives gives \(\int_0^1u^p(1-u)^qdu=p!q!/(p+q+1)!\). All boundary terms used in this calculation vanish except the final elementary integral. In characteristic zero every \(\beta_j\) is nonzero.

Consequently \(\deg I=d\) and \(I(0)=a_0\ne0\). Over an algebraic closure, choose a root \(h\ne0\) and put \(q=4/h\). Equation (7) vanishes by construction, and (9) gives the contradiction. A slice over \(k\) would remain one after field extension, so the obstruction applies to the original field as well.

If \(a_0=0\), the fixed-point argument applies. A nonzero constant gives the slice \(w/a_0\); the zero constant does not. This completes the classification for \(k[x]\).

### The two-variable case

Now write

\[
A(x,f)=\sum_{j=0}^d a_j(f)x^j
\]

with \(d>0\) and \(A(0,0)\ne0\). In \(k((e))[h]\), form

\[
I_e(h)=\sum_{j=0}^d \beta_j a_j(-e^2)h^j.
\tag{11}
\]

The substitution \(k[f]\to k[[e]]\), \(f\mapsto-e^2\), is injective. Thus this is a nonconstant polynomial with nonzero leading coefficient. Its constant coefficient is a unit of \(k[[e]]\).

Choose a root \(h\) in a finite algebraic extension \(L\) of \(k((e))\), and choose a valuation ring \(V\subset L\) dominating \(k[[e]]\). Such a ring exists by the valuation-ring domination lemma.[^3] The element \(h\) cannot belong to the maximal ideal of \(V\): if it did, every nonconstant term of (11) would lie in that ideal while its constant term would be a unit.

The valuation-ring dichotomy implies \(h^{-1}\in V\). This holds whether \(h\) is a unit of \(V\) or lies outside \(V\). Therefore \(q=4/h\) belongs to \(V\) and is nonzero. Both points (8) have coordinates in \(V\). However, \(qe^5\) lies in its maximal ideal, so \(T=2/(qe^5)\notin V\).

The orbit identities (5) through (9) remain valid over \(L\), and (11) makes the fourth-coordinate increment zero. A polynomial with coefficients in \(k\subset V\) evaluates into \(V\) at both endpoints. Equation (9) is again impossible. This proves the no-slice assertion whenever \(d>0\).

It remains to handle \(A=a(f)\) with no \(x\)-dependence. If it is nonconstant and \(a(0)\ne0\), choose a nonzero root \(c\) after extending the field to an algebraic closure. A hypothetical slice descends through the stable ideal \((f-c,w)\) to a slice on

\[
S_c=k[x,y,z]/(f-c)
\simeq k[x,r,g]/(xg-r^2-c^3).
\tag{12}
\]

The isomorphism retains \(x\) and uses the formulas for \(y,z\) in (3) with \(f=c\); it does not invert \(g\). The polynomial \(xg-r^2-c^3\) is irreducible by Gauss's lemma, so \(S_c\) is a domain and localization at \(g\) is injective. The induced derivation satisfies

\[
\delta g=0,\qquad\delta r=cg,\qquad\delta x=2cr.
\]

Its kernel is \(k[g]\). Indeed, localizing at \(g\) gives \(k[g,g^{-1},r]\), where the derivation is \(cg\partial_r\). A Laurent polynomial in \(g\) with a genuine pole at zero cannot belong to \(S_c\): multiplying by its minimal power of \(g\) and reducing modulo \(g\) would equate zero with a nonzero constant.

If \(\delta\) had a slice, the slice theorem would give \(S_c=k[g][s]\), so \(S_c/(g)\) would be a domain. But

\[
S_c/(g)=k[x,r]/(r^2+c^3)
\simeq k[x]\times k[x]
\]

over the algebraic closure, since \(c\ne0\). This contradiction finishes the proof for all \(a\in k[x,f]\).

## Classification for invariant coefficients in \(k[f,g]\)

Suppose first that \(a=c+fgP(f,g)\), where \(c\ne0\). Equations (2) immediately give

\[
D_a(w-Pr)=c.
\tag{13}
\]

This proves sufficiency and displays a slice. The coordinate change \(w'=w-Pr\) makes \(D_a=\Delta+c\partial_{w'}\). Form the finite polynomial flow \(\Phi_t\) of \(\Delta\) with an independent parameter \(t\), then substitute \(t=-w'/c\). Its three components, together with \(w'/c\), are a polynomial coordinate system. The inverse uses \(\Phi_{w'/c}\). This is substitution into a polynomial flow, not an assertion about exponentiating an arbitrary variable-coefficient derivation. The kernel is therefore a three-variable polynomial ring.

For necessity, a zero constant value at \((f,g)=(0,0)\) gives fixed points. Assume \(a(0,0)\ne0\), and extend scalars to an algebraic closure; a hypothetical slice survives this extension. If \(a(f,0)\) is nonconstant, choose a nonzero root \(c\). On the quotient \(f=c\), write \(a(c,g)=g b(g)\). The polynomial

\[
w'=w-\frac{b(g)}c r
\]

is invariant. A slice would descend further through \(w'=0\) to a slice on the surface (12), which has just been excluded.

The other axis is similar but has a different bad fiber. If \(a(0,g)\) is nonconstant, choose a nonzero root \(d\). On \(g=d\), write \(a(f,d)=f b(f)\), and set \(w'=w-b(f)r/d\). This is invariant. A slice would descend to the induced derivation on

\[
T_d=k[x,y,z]/(g-d).
\]

Here \(f\) is a non-zero-divisor. In the original UFD, \(f=xz-y^2\) is irreducible and does not divide \(g-d\), since the latter reduces modulo \(f\) to \(x^5-d\). Coprimality therefore shows that localization at \(f\) is injective. Formulas (3) give

\[
(T_d)_f\simeq k[f,f^{-1},r],\qquad
\delta=fd\partial_r.
\]

In particular \(T_d\) is a domain. The same Laurent-pole argument as before gives \(\ker\delta=k[f]\). Yet

\[
T_d/(f)
\simeq (k[x]/(x^5-d))[y]
\simeq\prod_{i=1}^{5}k[y]
\tag{14}
\]

over an algebraic closure: \(x\) is invertible, so \(z=y^2/x\), and \(x^5-d\) has five distinct roots. This is incompatible with a slice and the resulting identity \(T_d=k[f][s]\).

Both axis restrictions of \(a\) must consequently be constants. They agree at the origin, with common nonzero value \(c\). In the polynomial ring \(k[f,g]\), this says precisely that \(a-c\) is divisible by both \(f\) and \(g\), hence by \(fg\). The necessity and the classification follow.

For completeness, \(f,g\) are algebraically independent: their Jacobian derivation (1) is nonzero, so their differentials are independent over the fraction field in characteristic zero. The notation \(k[f,g]\) really is a polynomial coefficient ring.

## Limits of the family results

The two propositions do not classify all \(a\in k[x,y,z]\), nor all locally nilpotent derivations on four-space. Even their apparent extension to \(k[x,f,g]\) requires care. For example,

\[
\Delta(xr)=3fgx-2f^4,
\]

so the nonconstant coefficient \(a=1+3fgx-2f^4\) gives the explicit slice \(w-xr\). A rule excluding every coefficient with \(x\)-dependence would therefore be false.

More generally, replacing \(a\) by \(a-\Delta H\) corresponds to the polynomial coordinate change \(w\mapsto w-H\). Any future classification should account for this equivalence before searching a large coefficient space. A claim that all slice extensions are equivalent to constant extensions remains an unproved proposal here, not a consequence of the two propositions.

One additional coefficient outside the two families can be excluded exactly: \(a=1+y\). Along (4), using \(y=-(r+x^3)/f\),

\[
y(uT)=e(1-2u)+\frac{64}{q^3e^2}(u^2-u)^3,
\qquad
\int_0^1 y(uT)du=-\frac{16}{35q^3e^2}.
\]

Choose an algebraic constant \(C\) with \(C^3=16/35\), and set \(e=H^3\), \(q=CH^{-2}\). Then the fourth-coordinate displacement for \(1+y\) is zero. The endpoints are \((0,\pm H^3,CH^4,0)\), whereas the translation time is \((2/C)H^{-13}\). The same polynomial-pole contradiction excludes a slice in every degree. This is one additional exact example, not a classification of all coefficients depending on \(y\).

The family results rule out one particular strategy for constructing a cancellation counterexample. They do not show that the general conjecture is more likely to be true, do not bound the degree of a possible counterexample, and do not produce an exotic threefold whose cylinder is affine four-space.

## Other routes and their remaining proof gaps

### Filtered invariant lifting

A common proposed strategy is to simplify a derivation by taking its highest weighted part, find an invariant coordinate there, and lift it back. Two separate claims are involved: lifting invariance, and retaining the polynomial-coordinate property. They must not be combined into an unsupported implication.

For strictly positive integer weights, the second claim has a simple sufficient criterion. If the highest weighted part of a polynomial is a coordinate, its differential at the origin is nonzero. One variable of that exact weight must occur linearly with a nonzero scalar coefficient. Positivity prevents any other occurrence of that variable, even in the lower-weight terms. The original polynomial is therefore a coordinate with an explicit elementary inverse.

The first claim fails. For \(D=\partial_x+x\partial_y\), the leading derivation is \(x\partial_y\), which fixes \(x\). But no polynomial with highest part \(x\) is invariant under \(D\). In the family \(D_h=h\partial_x+x\partial_y\), attempting a lift \(x+ha_1\) already requires \(x\partial_ya_1=-1\) at first order, which is impossible. This is consistent with the standard inclusion \(\operatorname{gr}(\ker D)\subseteq\ker(\operatorname{gr}D)\), which need not be equality.[^4]

There is a second limitation. The polynomial coordinates \(a=x+y^2\), \(b=y+a^2\) conjugate a translation to a nonlinear derivation with kernel \(k[b]\). No nonconstant polynomial in \(b\) has a nonzero constant directional derivative. Therefore no invariant has a coordinate highest part under any positive weights, even after any linear coordinate change. Nonlinear coordinate adaptation can be essential even in a completely solved example. The detailed proof, finite lifting criterion, and a separate formal-versus-polynomial inverse counterexample are in [the filtered-route note](research/lifting-route.md).

### Nice derivations and straight-line orbits

For a derivation \(D=\sum F_i\partial_i\) satisfying \(D^2x_i=0\), the equation \(JF\,F=0\) makes the action a quasi-translation \(x\mapsto x+tF(x)\). A slice \(s\) then gives the explicit invariant retraction \(G=x-sF\). This produces a hypersurface with affine-line cylinder, but it does not prove that hypersurface is affine space. De Bondt's classification handles homogeneous quasi-translations in four variables.[^5]

The nonhomogeneous quadratic case can be narrowed. The known triangularization theorem for nilpotent quadratic Jacobians of rank at most two supplies a linear invariant coordinate or a linear slice.[^6] A potential survivor outside these results must have generic Jacobian rank three, and, if it were a cancellation counterexample, full derivation rank four. These are different ranks.

Low-degree slices impose a further restriction, independent of niceness. Every polynomial slice of degree at most two is a coordinate. Write it as \(s=c+\ell^Tx+\tfrac12x^TSx\), with \(S\) symmetric. Since \(D(s)=1\), its gradient \(\ell+Sx\) has no zero, so \(\ell\notin\operatorname{im}S\). Linear algebra gives \(v\in\ker S\) with \(\ell^Tv\ne0\). The directional derivative \(\partial_vs\) is that nonzero constant. In a linear basis beginning with \(v\), the polynomial is linear in the first variable with constant coefficient and independent of it otherwise, giving an explicit inverse. Therefore a hypothetical counterexample cannot have a slice of degree at most two.

The [nice-derivation note](research/nice-route.md) records a bounded coefficient system beginning with a cubic slice. No surviving example, impossibility proof for that packet, or completeness bound covering higher-degree slices is established here.

### Exotic cylinders and invariant failure

Dubouloz and Ghosh construct families of nonisomorphic exotic varieties with isomorphic cylinders. Their cylinder isomorphisms do not identify those cylinders with affine space. The construction mixes the cylinder variable into the old coordinate ring while preserving some distinguished coordinates.[^7] Thus preserving a divisor is weaker than preserving the unstabilized subring needed for a cancellation argument.

For the Russell cubic \(A=\mathbb C[x,y,z,t]/(x^2y+z^2+t^3+x)\), a hypothetical polynomial presentation of \(A[w]\) would transport \(x\) to a polynomial on affine four-space with affine-three-space nonzero fibers and a nonnormal cusp-cylinder zero fiber. Ruling out that particular configuration would be meaningful. Merely showing that its general fibers are affine spaces does not rule out the exceptional fiber.

A proposed stronger invariant is already known not to help. Gaifullin and Shafarevich prove that the intersection of kernels of slice derivations equals the ordinary Makar-Limanov invariant whenever any slice exists. They explicitly apply the result to the Russell cubic cylinder.[^8] Thus restricting attention to slice actions does not restore the lost invariant. The [cylinder-route note](research/cylinder-route.md) records the precise distinctions between base isomorphisms, cylinder isomorphisms, and affine-space recognition.

Vénéreau polynomials pose another scope trap. The unresolved coordinate questions around that family concern ambient embeddings and relative coordinates; an already polynomial zero fiber is not itself an exotic cancellation base. Existing results on bivariables and stable coordinates must be kept separate from a proof that a given four-variable polynomial is an ambient coordinate.[^9]

## Literature and originality status

The rank-three derivation and its local-slice structure come from established work. The two coefficient-family propositions have complete arguments in this note, but a complete priority determination has not been made. A failure to locate the exact displayed statement is not evidence that it is new. General theorems about extensions, plinth ideals, and orbit separation may subsume it in a different formulation.

The closest audited extension results are Freudenburg's general-extension Proposition 7.1 and fundamental-derivation Theorem 7.6.[^10] They impose additional hypotheses and describe invariant rings or degree modules; neither was verified to state the two slice criteria above. In particular, a theorem about fundamental derivations must not be applied to an arbitrary rank-three derivation without establishing the required structure. The necessity directions here rest on the displayed orbit and fiber arguments.

The modified Makar-Limanov calculation illustrates the risk concretely: it can be reconstructed by a short argument and still be a special case explicitly addressed by a published theorem. The same standard applies to the extension classifications. Their appropriate current status is **proved in this working note, originality unestablished, no expert review**.

The next useful mathematical task is to understand coefficients modulo \(\Delta(k[x,y,z])\), since subtracting such an image is a polynomial change of the added variable. A bounded next question is whether a specified larger coefficient family can have a slice without reducing to a constant coefficient. It must be tested against both the orbit obstruction and the positive example \(1+\Delta(xr)\). No answer to the arbitrary-coefficient question is asserted here.

## Exact verification and reproducibility

The accompanying `verify_extension.py` uses only standard-library rational arithmetic and an independently implemented sparse Laurent-polynomial representation. It imports neither the exploratory linear solver nor SymPy. Its checks reconstruct the Jacobian derivation, the invariant relations, the localization identities, nilpotence on generators, all four orbit endpoints, and deliberate wrong-time and arithmetic negative controls.

The all-degree arguments remain written proofs. The checker does not quantify over all polynomials, prove the valuation-ring existence theorem, or constitute a Lean formalization. In particular, checking the integral coefficients through degree twelve supports the implementation but is not the justification for their all-degree formula; the integration-by-parts proof supplies that justification.

Reproduction commands, from this directory:

```sh
python3 verify_extension.py
/opt/anaconda3/bin/python verify_exact.py
/opt/anaconda3/bin/python research/rank_three_extension.py --degree 16 --a 1+x
```

The bounded search includes every polynomial of the stated total degree over the rational numbers. For \(a=1+x\) and degree at most 16, it has 4,845 unknown coefficients, 10,071 monomial equations, and augmented rank 4,823; the final augmented column is a pivot, so there is no solution in that space. This finite result is consistent with, but superseded by, the all-degree orbit proof. No incomplete computation is counted as an exclusion.
